01 The myth

The myth

One sentence, two claims, tested separately.

"따릉이 is mainly for leisure and exercise, not a real commuting mode, so subway fare changes will not meaningfully change how it is used."

The sentence makes two claims. We test them separately, because the conclusion can be right for the wrong reason.

The premise

Bikes are a leisure mode, not a real way to commute.

Tested in chapter 06

The conclusion

So a fare rise leaves the commuting-hour share of rides unchanged.

Tested in chapters 03–05

Our hypothesis, fixed before we saw the data

  • H₀: the mean weekday commuting-hour share at commute-zone stations is the same before and after a fare rise.
  • H₁: it is different (two-sided, α = 0.05). A fall would contradict the myth as much as a rise.
  • What counts as meaningful: a change of 3 percentage points or more. If the whole 95% confidence interval sits inside ±3 points, the myth's conclusion holds.

02 The investigation

The investigation

The trick was to measure what people ride for, not how much they ride.

Total rentals swing with the weather by a factor of six between a freezing January day and a mild autumn one. A fare-driven switch from subway to bike would be a small ripple in that total. It would show up in the composition: the share of weekday rides that start in commuting hours (07:00–08:59 and 18:00–19:59).

The data, and the obstacles

Sources

  • Seoul Open Data Plaza: hourly rental records (12 monthly files, 200–450 MB each), daily rental counts for 2022, the station list (2,789 stations)

Obstacles we got past

  • Files too large for Google Drive: condensed one at a time in Colab to weekday × station rows

  • The subway-station coordinate file has no download: built zones from station names instead

Check: November 2022 reproduces our Week 3 pilot exactly (mean share 32.86%, SD 1.80). 96.6% of rentals match a station in the station list.

Each bike = 10,000 rides (multiple regression). The 95% range is a prediction interval for a single day. Compare the two models on a rainy weekend: the simple model has no way to know.

What each control adds

Leaving rain out hid part of the heat effect

Hot days are wetter in Seoul (monsoon). Temperature alone gives 3,183 rentals per °C; with rain in the model it is 3,647. The gap is the classic omitted-variable bias: rain rises 0.36 mm per °C and each mm costs about 1,291 rides.

Our main model now

rentals = β₀ + β₁·temp + β₂·temp² + β₃·temp³ + β₄·log(1 + rain) + β₅·rest day. It explains 86% of day-to-day variation (46% for temperature alone), and 86% on days it never saw. On a dry workday, use peaks near 22 °C.

Model checks
  • All five terms significant (p < 0.001), using HAC standard errors because days are autocorrelated (Durbin–Watson 1.41) and the spread grows with volume (Breusch–Pagan p < 0.001).
  • Why temperature³: the quadratic model left an S-shaped pattern in its errors, about 18,000 rides too low below −5 °C and 16,000 too high at 0–5 °C. The cubic term removes it.
  • Temperature is centred before taking powers; the largest VIF is 7.05, below the usual limit of 10.
  • Adding month dummies lifts held-out R² only slightly (0.87 against 0.86) and shrinks the temperature effect by a third, because months absorb part of the temperature variation. It is a sensitivity check, not the main model.
  • Hours of rain add another 4 points of R² (0.90). That variable was found by the machine-learning map in chapter 07.

05 Is it causal?

Is it causal?

"No change after the fare rise" is a correlation. To say the fare caused no change, we need a stand-in for the Seoul where the fare did not rise.

The risk with a null result is masking: a real +2 point fare effect could hide under a −2 point shift from something else. Each design below uses a different stand-in, so each rules out a different kind of masking.

Assumption checks

    What we can and cannot claim

    • Can: under parallel trends, the fare rises raised the commute-zone share by at most about 2 points, and probably less than 1.
    • Can: the commute zone changed like any random group of stations, so there is no sign of subway substitution near exits.
    • Cannot: say whether individuals switched modes (no person-level data), or rule out effects under 1 point.

    Why "non-significant" can still matter

    • A capped supply hides demand. Exit stations can run out of bikes at 8 a.m.; extra demand would not show up as extra rentals.
    • Averages cancel. Long-distance commuters and flat-pass holders may react in opposite directions.
    • Our zones are blunt. Name-based zones differ by only 1–3 points, which dilutes any zone-specific effect.
    • Linear tests miss shapes. Rain is not significant for the share in the regression, yet the random forest ranks it near the top.

    If policy ignores the hidden features

    • Fare planning that expects a bike spill-over would over-supply exit stations that are actually limited by morning rebalancing.
    • Judging fare policy without the Climate Card would miss that flat-pass riders feel no price change at all.
    • Demand forecasts built on daily rain totals would miss commuting-hour showers, which matter more than millimetres.
    08 What it means

    What it means

    The answer, and what to do with it.

    따릉이 is not mainly a leisure toy. It carries a third of weekday rides in commuting hours. But it behaves like a fair-weather option, not a subway substitute, so fare rises leave it alone. Weather, not fares, moves the bikes.

    For transport policy

    Do not count on fare rises to push commuters onto public bikes. If Seoul wants more bike commuting, the levers are the conditions that make riders give up: rain, cold and empty docks at peak hours.

    For bike operations

    Temperature, rain and the calendar explain 86% of daily demand. Schedule rebalancing and maintenance around the weather forecast rather than around fare announcements.

    Call to action

    The next study should join peak-hour bike availability at subway-exit stations and Climate Card use to this data. Those are the two hidden features most likely to mask a fare effect, and both can be measured.

    Your call

    You get one more dataset. Which hidden feature would you measure first?

    09 How we worked

    How we worked

    Five weeks, one decision at a time.

    What data-driven decision making looked like here

    Try it · ride through the weeks
    • Week 2: chose the myth, defined "meaningful" (3 points) before seeing any data.
    • Week 3: picked the weekday as the unit and a two-sided test with an equivalence rule.
    • Week 4: planned a temperature regression to remove the biggest confounder.
    • Week 5: collected 12 large files, built zones, ran the tests, then added causal checks.
    • Week 6: moved to multiple regression after the professor's feedback and built the three maps.

    Lessons about statistical thinking

    • "Not significant" is not "no effect". Only the equivalence test lets us say the effect is smaller than 3 points.
    • The unit decides the answer. Counted per ride, a 0.8-point change is "significant" (χ² p < 0.001); counted per day, it is not.
    • Controls move estimates. Leaving rain out shrank the temperature effect by 13%.
    • A design is only as good as its comparison group. The leisure zone turned a weak signal (Mann–Whitney p = 0.04, one-sided) into a citywide drift.

    Limitations and next steps

    • Two planned checks need more data: a no-fare-change placebo year (2021 vs 2022) and subway ridership by hour.
    • Zones come from station names; coordinate-based zones would separate commuting and leisure more sharply.
    • Commuting hours are a proxy, and daily rain totals do not say when it rained.
    • The 2025 rise was small (+5.2%) and Climate Card holders did not feel it.
